Florida Window Tint Law (2026): What's Legal?

Window tint keeps your car cooler, blocks UV, and cuts glare — but in Florida, how dark you can legally go is set by state law (Florida Statutes 316.2951–316.2957). Here's what's legal in 2026, so you can pick a shade that looks great and passes a traffic stop.

What is VLT?

Florida measures tint by VLT — Visible Light Transmission, the percentage of visible light the window lets through. A higher number means lighter tint; a lower number means darker. So "more than 28% VLT" means the film must let at least 28% of light in — you cannot go darker than that limit on those windows. Factory glass already blocks some light, so the tint plus the glass together must meet the limit.

Legal tint limits for cars in Florida

The rules differ between passenger cars (sedans) and larger vehicles like SUVs, vans, and multipurpose vehicles. Here are the front-and-back limits for each:

WindowSedan limitSUV & Van limit
Front side windowsMore than 28% VLTMore than 28% VLT
Back side windowsMore than 15% VLTAny darkness allowed
Rear windowMore than 15% VLTAny darkness allowed
WindshieldNon-reflective, above AS-1 lineNon-reflective, above AS-1 line

In short: on any vehicle, your front side windows must let more than 28% of light through. Sedans are also limited to more than 15% on the back glass, while SUVs and vans can run the back side windows and rear window as dark as you like.

Windshield tint rules

Florida allows non-reflective tint on the windshield only above the manufacturer's AS-1 line — a small etched mark near the top of the glass, usually a few inches down from the roof. This top strip (often called a visor or eyebrow strip) helps cut sun glare. Applying tint to the full windshield below the AS-1 line is not permitted on a standard vehicle without a medical exemption.

What about reflective or mirrored tint?

Beyond darkness, Florida also limits how much a film can mirror or reflect light. Front side windows may be no more than 25% reflective, and back side windows no more than 35% reflective. That's why heavily mirrored or chrome-look films are generally off the table for street use, even if the darkness would otherwise be legal.

Medical exemptions

Florida allows darker tint than the standard limits for drivers with an approved medical exemption certificate for a qualifying light-sensitive condition. If you have one, keep the certificate in the vehicle so you can show it during a stop. Ask your physician about the current application process, and bring your paperwork to us so we can install the shade your exemption allows.

What happens if my tint is too dark?

Tint that violates the limits is generally cited as a nonmoving violation in Florida, and an officer can require you to remove the illegal film. Some situations may be handled as a moving violation depending on the circumstances. Exact fine amounts and how strictly the rules are enforced vary by county and citing agency, so check current Florida Statutes or your local court for the specifics that apply to you.

Please note: tint laws can change, and enforcement varies. Always verify the current Florida Statutes (316.2951–316.2957) or ask us before you commit to a shade — we install to current legal limits by default so you don't have to worry about it.
